# crimes — 8.6 million rows, end to end

The largest workstream in the gallery, and the closest thing to a
whole-engine benchmark: the complete City of Chicago crime record
(2001—present, ~2 GB of live CSV, 8.6 million rows) downloaded as part
of the run, profiled, mined, and modeled — with every algorithm,
including the machine learning, written in olang itself.

    olang run main.ol

The first run downloads ~2 GB (cached under `data/`, atomic and
re-verified); a cached run takes a few minutes end to end, most of it
the honest compute of training a model on hundreds of thousands of
rows. `data/` and `out/` are regenerated by the run and ignored by git.

## The stages

1. **fetch** — the full extract, with retries, a 1.5 GB sanity floor,
   and atomic writes.
2. **load** — 8.6M x 22 through the general (quoted-CSV) parser,
   profiled for null coverage.
3. **derive** — month and hour parsed out of 8.6 million timestamp
   strings by one bulk lambda each.
4. **trend** — incidents per year with a fitted linear decline
   (R² ≈ 0.9 over two decades).
5. **categories** — the 34 primary types, ranked, with per-type arrest
   rates.
6. **rhythm** — the hourly and monthly pulse, with the midnight
   data-entry artifact named rather than hidden.
7. **chi-square** — arrest x domestic-flag independence, rejected at
   χ² ≈ 33,000.
8. **k-means** (in olang) — Lloyd's algorithm over ~900k recent
   geocoded incidents, k=10.
9. **logistic regression** (in olang) — arrest prediction from time,
   place, and category features on 2025— incidents: ~305k training
   rows x 11 features x 100 epochs of full-batch gradient descent,
   with held-out accuracy, precision/recall, AUC, and the loss curve
   as proof of convergence.
10. **report** — `out/report.md`, five SVG charts, three derived CSVs.

## Why it is a benchmark

Every stage leans on a different part of the engine: the CSV reader at
2 GB, bulk lambdas over 8.6M cells, group-bys and joins at full scale,
and the ML loops — deliberately written as ordinary olang functions —
run millions of iterations through the bytecode tier. The stage
timings printed by the run are the scoreboard.

## Data

[City of Chicago — Crimes 2001 to Present](https://data.cityofchicago.org/Public-Safety/Crimes-2001-to-Present/ijzp-q8t2),
public domain. Incidents with unknown time are stamped midnight in the
source; the report calls this out where it shows.
